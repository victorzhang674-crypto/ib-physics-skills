# Physics IA section checklist

Practical checks for planning, writing and reviewing a report, drawn from common teacher feedback on high-scoring exemplars. Schools often issue their own template and conventions. When the student supplies them, follow the teacher's version and note where it differs from these defaults.

## Research design

- **Research question.** Put it in the title or directly under it. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable and the specific system or condition that makes the investigation unique, for example "How does the number of stacked coffee filters affect the terminal speed of the filter stack falling through air?" Restate it before the background section if the title is long.
- **Background.** Explain only the physics the analysis uses and lead it to a testable relation, ideally one that predicts a gradient or intercept. Syllabus equations need no derivation. A hypothesis is optional but makes the conclusion easier to write.
- **Justify every choice.** For each decision, give what was done, how, and why: the choice of system, range and its upper and lower limits, number and spacing of increments, number of repeats, instruments and settings. A pilot result is the strongest justification.
- **Variables.** Give units for each variable. For each controlled variable, state why it matters and how it was held constant and checked. List them in order of importance.
- **Equipment.** Give each measuring instrument's range and smallest division or resolution, plus model, mass, dimensions or settings needed for reproduction, such as camera frame rate, resolution and scale reference for video analysis.
- **Setup.** Include a labelled diagram with a caption. A photo can supplement it but does not replace it.
- **Method.** Write numbered steps in the past tense and passive voice with specific values. Avoid repetition and fold reasons into the step where they matter. Include actions that reduced uncertainty, such as alignment checks, repeats and calibration.
- **Safety, ethics and environment.** Cover the issues that apply to the method briefly and specifically.

## Data volume

Aim for at least 5 independent-variable values with at least 3 trials each. Many teachers recommend about 7 or more values with 5 trials. Use more values when the expected relationship is curved or the range is wide.

## Data analysis

- **Raw data table.** Caption above, units and uncertainty in each heading, decimal places consistent with the uncertainty, values in order. Record qualitative observations too.
- **Uncertainty of a reading.** Use the smallest division for a digital instrument or half of it for an analogue scale, unless the measurement process justifies a larger value. For repeats, use half the range, or the teacher's preferred measure. Use the larger of the instrument and repeat uncertainties and justify any increase.
- **Rounding.** Give uncertainties to 1 significant figure (2 if the first digit is 1 when the teacher allows it) and match the value's decimal places, for example 5.0 ± 0.1 g. Keep full precision in intermediate calculations.
- **Worked examples.** Show one example for each non-trivial calculation, including the propagated uncertainty.
- **Graphs.** Use scatter plots with captions below. Label axes with the quantity in words and its unit, transformed units included. Add error bars in both directions or state that one direction is negligible. Do not force the fit through the origin.
- **Linearization.** Plot the raw data first and comment on its shape. Then linearize according to the model and propagate uncertainty into the transformed column.
- **Gradient and intercept uncertainty.** Draw maximum and minimum lines consistent with the error bars, usually through opposite corners of the first and last bars. State the convention used and apply it consistently. Common conventions are half the difference between the maximum and minimum gradients, or the largest deviation from the best-fit gradient. If no line passes through every bar, say so. Report gradient and intercept with uncertainty and units.

## Conclusion

- Answer the research question directly with the experimental relationship, numbers, uncertainties and units, and the range studied.
- Interpret the gradient, the intercept and any area under the graph physically. A nonzero intercept often signals a systematic effect, so estimate and explain it.
- Compare with a cited accepted value, published result or theoretical prediction, such as a slope derived from the model. State whether the agreement holds within the uncertainty and what that implies about accuracy and precision. Cite the conclusion of the source being compared.
- Do not use R² or a correlation coefficient as evidence that the model is correct.

## Evaluation

- Discuss weaknesses (affecting the measured data) and limitations (restricting the scope of the conclusion). Strengths are optional.
- For each issue, give the evidence, the direction of the effect on the data, gradient or intercept, and its relative significance. Rank the issues.
- Give a specific, feasible improvement for each issue and explain how it addresses that issue. "Use a light gate" alone is not specific enough.
- Exclude data-processing mistakes and generic remarks about large uncertainties. Add an extension that tests the conclusion further.

## Presentation

Number and caption all tables and figures and refer to them in the text. Keep tables from breaking across pages. Prefer one comparison graph to several similar graphs. Keep essential content in the main report because appendices may not be read.

## Video and motion-tracking measurements

Record the frame rate, resolution, a scale reference in the plane of motion and the camera's distance and alignment. Treat time resolution as one frame interval unless timing over many frames justifies less. Estimate position uncertainty from the scale and tracking. Address parallax and motion blur. For terminal or steady-state speed, show that the analyzed interval is past the acceleration phase, for example with a position–time graph that has become linear.
